## Key Definitions

- **Q-Submission (Q-Sub)**: A mechanism to request different types of interactions with FDA. We often abbreviate the word "submission" and refer to this as a Q-Sub.
- **Pre-Submission (Pre-Sub)**: One specific type of Q-Submission, often abbreviated as a Pre-Sub.
- **Premarket Approval Application (PMA)**: A mechanism to request approval for a Class 3 medical device.
- **Investigational Device Exemption (IDE)**: A mechanism to request approval for a significant risk clinical study of an unapproved device or unapproved use of a device.
- **Investigational New Drug (IND)**: A mechanism to request a drug or biological drug be used in a clinical investigation.

## History of the Q-Submission Program

- **1995**: A Pre-IDE program was established, providing a way to request feedback about future IDE submissions.
- Over time, as the utility of these submissions became evident, FDA started receiving requests for feedback to discuss planned submissions of other types such as PMAs or 510(k)s.
- **2013**: The program was formally reestablished as the Pre-Submission or Pre-Sub Program and was expanded to include requests for other submission types in addition to IDEs. A formal guidance document was issued in 2014.
- **Current**: The program has continued to grow and include different types of feedback requests in addition to Pre-Subs, evolving into the Q-Submission or Q-Sub Program.

## What is the Q-Submission Program?

The Q-Submission Program is a voluntary program that provides a mechanism to request different types of interactions with FDA regarding medical device submissions. The request that comes into the FDA is called a Q-Submission, or a Q-Sub, and can request interactions to discuss a number of different topics and request different types of feedback.

Types of interactions that can be requested:

- **Feedback about potential or planned medical device submissions** (one type of Q-Sub)
- **Certain formal determinations** that are not standalone marketing submissions or research authorizations, such as accessory classifications or study-risk determinations

Depending on the topic and the submitter's preference, these requested interactions can consist of:

- Written feedback
- A face-to-face meeting
- A teleconference

## Scope of the Guidance Document

The guidance document explains:

- The purpose for each of the different Q-Sub types
- Q-Sub processes including submission content, feedback mechanisms, meeting format and documentation via minutes, and review timelines
- This information is provided for the main Q-Sub types
- Other Q-Sub types are discussed in less detail, with references to other guidance documents

There are also some requests for feedback that do not fall into the scope of any specific Q-Sub types. Even though they are outside the scope, these requests are still tracked by FDA as Q-Submissions. The guidance discusses this process and outlines examples.

## Main Q-Submission Types

The five most common Q-Subs (the "main Q-Subs") are discussed in most detail:

1. **Pre-Sub meeting requests**
2. **Pre-Sub written feedback requests**
3. **Submission Issue Requests (SIRs)**
4. **Informational meeting requests**
5. **Study risk determinations**

### Other Q-Sub Types

The following are described more extensively in their own corresponding guidance documents:

- PMA Day 100 Meetings
- Breakthrough Device Designation requests
- Interaction for Breakthrough Devices
- Early Collaboration meetings
- Accessory Classification requests

## Pre-Submissions

Pre-Submissions are the most common type of Q-Sub. A Pre-Sub is a formal request to FDA for feedback that will be provided in the form of a written response, and possibly a subsequent meeting, prior to an intended premarket submission, IDE, accessory classification request or CLIA Waiver.

### Types of Pre-Submissions

Depending on the type of interaction requested:

1. **Pre-Sub meeting request**: FDA provides written feedback followed by a meeting (either teleconference or face-to-face)
2. **Pre-Sub written feedback request**: FDA provides only written feedback

Both serve the same purpose and can be used to discuss issues relevant to a planned marketing submission, IDE, or CLIA Waiver.

### Pre-Submissions Can Discuss

Pre-Subs are submitted to discuss potential or planned:

- IDEs
- PMAs
- HDEs
- De Novo requests
- 510(k)s
- Duals
- BLAs
- INDs
- Accessory Classification requests
- CLIA Waivers

Pre-Submissions are used to help guide product development, develop study protocols, or prepare an application. Specific questions should be included in Pre-Submissions so FDA can provide useful feedback.

### Best Practices for Pre-Submissions

The most productive conversations occur when Pre-Submissions include focused questions and do not try to cover too many different topics. The new guidance recommends that Pre-Submissions are limited to **three to four substantial topics** in order to have the most useful meetings.

### MDUFA Commitments

Pre-Submissions are the most common type of Q-Submission and the only type that have MDUFA commitments. Key commitments include:

- Goal times for providing feedback
- Submitter responsibility for drafting meeting minutes
- FDA intends feedback provided in response to a Pre-Sub will not change, provided the information submitted in a future IDE or marketing submission is consistent with that provided in the Pre-Sub, and that the data or other information in the future submission do not raise any important new issue materially affecting safety or effectiveness

The guidance document includes:

- A new Pre-Submission checklist in Appendix 1
- Examples of questions that can lead to productive interactions in Appendix 2

## Submission Issue Requests (SIRs)

Submission Issue Requests are the next most common type of Q-Submission. These are requests to discuss outstanding review issues that were provided in a marketing submission hold letter, IDE letter, or an IND clinical hold letter. Either written feedback or a meeting is requested.

### When SIRs Are Not Necessary

- Simple questions about clarification of issues in a letter where the involvement of management is not needed
- Minor clarification questions or administrative issues that can be addressed by the lead reviewer
- Discussing issues while a file is under active review (this would be done via interactive review)

### Purpose

Submission Issue Requests can help submitters determine how to respond to outstanding questions in formal responses. Before the new Q-Sub guidance, this type of Q-Sub was called a Submission Issue Meeting, or SIM. Although the name changed, they still provide the same purpose: to help move a project forward by facilitating interactions with FDA and discussing approaches to address deficiencies.

## Study Risk Determinations

Study Risk Determinations are used when a submitter is interested in obtaining a significant risk or non-significant risk determination for a future clinical study. FDA reviews these requests and provides a final determination in writing as to whether the proposed clinical study is:

- Exempt from IDE regulations
- Basic physiological research
- Non-significant risk
- Significant risk

For non-exempt studies, the regulation specifies that sponsors are responsible for making the initial risk determination and presenting it to an IRB. FDA is available to help make that risk determination, and FDA is the final arbiter as to whether a device study is significant or non-significant risk.

## Informational Meetings

Informational Meetings are requests to present information to the FDA without the expectation of official feedback. These meetings can be used to:

- Provide an overview of ongoing device development when multiple submissions are planned in quick succession
- Introduce new technology to the FDA review team

While the reason for these meetings can vary, their intent is to share information with the FDA and provide an opportunity for interactive dialogue. Although FDA does not provide written feedback, they can ask questions during the meeting and may offer suggestions regarding future submissions. As with other types of Q-Submission meetings, these are all documented with meeting minutes.

Informational Meetings can also be used to document requests that don't fall within the scope of any type of Q-Submission. When used this way, Informational Meetings can include FDA feedback. The guidance document includes examples of interactions that may fall outside the scope of an existing Q-Submission type, but may be tracked as an Informational Meeting Q-Sub.

## Significant Changes in the New Guidance

### Separation of Pre-Submission Types

Since there are additional goals when a meeting is requested (and therefore a different review process), Pre-Submissions have been separated into:

- Pre-Submission meeting requests
- Pre-Submission written feedback requests

The guidance separates these accordingly when discussing timelines and processes, and requests that Pre-Submissions specify if they are requesting written feedback only or a meeting.

### MDUFA Milestones

- Completing an acceptance review (RTA) within **15 days** of receiving a Pre-Submission
- For Pre-Submission meeting requests: determining a meeting date by **Day 30**, and providing written feedback **5 days before a meeting** or by **Day 70**, whichever is sooner

### Acceptance Review Changes

- Submission Issue Requests and Informational Meetings no longer receive an RTA review
- Updated, streamlined RTA checklist for Pre-Submissions (Appendix 1)

### Submission Issue Request Changes

- Renamed from Submission Issue Meetings (SIMs) to Submission Issue Requests (SIRs)
- Feedback can now be either written feedback or a meeting (submission should include preferred feedback mechanism)
- **Two-tiered review timeline**: Based on how much time has passed between when the associated letter was sent and the Submission Issue Request was received:
  - **Within 60 days**: FDA will aim to provide feedback within **21 days**
  - **More than 60 days**: FDA will aim to provide feedback in **70 days**

## Summary

- The Q-Submission Program has been extended to include a variety of submission types that all provide a mechanism for submitters to request FDA interactions
- Each type of Q-Submission has its own review process and timeline
- The new Q-Submission guidance document describes the various Q-Sub types and corresponding timelines, and provides resources for Q-Submission types with policy and procedures described in more detail in other FDA guidance documents

### Common to All Q-Submissions

- All Q-Submissions regardless of type should be formally submitted in electronic copy format to FDA via the document control center
- Related Q-Submissions are all tracked with supplements and amendments, regardless of Q-Sub type
- The guidance describes meeting formats and submission of meeting minutes

## Questions and Answers

### Why was the SIR timeline changed to a two-tier system?

Susannah Gilbert: The FDA wants to encourage a shorter hold time and prompt resolution that will support achievement of the MDUFA shared outcome goals for total times and decisions. When Submission Issue Requests are received soon after a hold letter or IDE letter is sent, the issues being discussed are still current. In contrast, when a Submission Issue Request is received many months after the review is completed and a letter was sent, the review team may have to refamiliarize themselves with the issues previously discussed. This additional step adds time to the overall process.

### Q: With 60 days + 70 days = 130 days and 510(k) response due by 180 days, what if we're past Day 80?

Susannah Gilbert: That's a hard question and depends on the type of questions. If it has taken you that long to come up with the questions and realize there are issues, chances are it's going to take longer to review as well. I always think it's a good idea to come in and hope that the time will be shorter.

Josh Nipper: It's perfectly okay to reach out to the lead reviewer of the review management and discuss expectations. 70 days is our goal -- the maximum. If it's a single question, the review division may have resources to do it before 70 days. If you're asking 10 or 15 follow-up questions on a very long deficiency letter and you wait until it's essentially very close to being due, you do run the risk of having the 510(k) deleted.

### Q: What about concurrent pre-submissions for the same product on different topics?

Susannah Gilbert: If you come in with one and have questions on another topic at a later time, you're absolutely allowed to come in. Submissions for the same devices with the same intended uses are tracked as supplements and kept in the same Q-Sub family so the review team can keep the information together. But if you submit them when they overlap, the review team might still be working on the previous submission and might not have resources for a different team to work on the next one.

Josh Nipper: If you have questions that close together in time, it would be better to include those in a more comprehensive pre-submission. For a meeting, we ask that the number of questions be limited -- three or four main topics is the best. We should not have multiple pre-submissions for the same product and same indication open at the same time. If you had the same device and two completely different indications, that would be a different case.

### Q: Can two devices with the same intended use (monitoring different disease stages) be addressed in one PMA or require separate submissions?

Josh Nipper: If you have a simple question about the best way to submit, reach out to the lead reviewer or management you've been dealing with. We don't want every question coming to CDRH to be a Q-Sub. If it's a complicated question needing additional analysis, a reviewer may say to submit a Pre-Sub. The specific case described sounds like a Pre-Sub could be useful.

### Q: Is it appropriate to use a Pre-Sub meeting for questions about regulatory pathways for AI-related products that may not be regulated?

Susannah Gilbert: My recommendation would be to reach out to the team within your area. If they think it would be helpful to talk about this, they can recommend you to the specific support team.

Josh Nipper: There's the 513G program for "am I a medical device" or "what classification am I" questions. We fully support sending in pre-submissions or information meetings to present your product and get feedback on the development plan. Under the Pre-Sub program, you wouldn't likely get a formal decision, but you may get general suggestions or feedback on whether your software would be regulated.

### Q: Will FDA provide written feedback if a meeting was requested for an SIR?

Susannah Gilbert: The standard from now on is going to be either request a meeting or request written feedback -- separate and one way or the other. There may be certain exceptions depending on the situation or the review team's resources.

Meeting minutes are always required if there's a meeting.

### Q: If written feedback was requested but a meeting is later desired, is there a channel?

Susannah Gilbert: Yes, usually this depends on the resources of the review team. If a meeting hasn't been planned ahead of time, it might take longer to schedule.

Josh Nipper: My general recommendation is to request the meeting up front. It's easier to cancel a meeting that's already on the books than it is to add one that hasn't been planned. We see companies get the feedback and say they don't need the meeting -- and the meeting is canceled. That's perfectly okay.

### Q: If Pre-Sub feedback disagrees with least burdensome principles, can we raise that before the actual submission?

Josh Nipper: We do not have the least burdensome flag in place for Pre-Subs. It's perfectly fine to discuss it within the actual submission (510(k) or PMA). There's no plan to create an appeal process for the Pre-Sub, because it is our best feedback at the time.

It's perfectly acceptable to reach out to management -- the assistant director (former branch chief) or division director. A lot of pre-submission feedback is reviewed by management. If you're at the submission issue phase for a 510(k), you have the option to throw the least burdensome flag. For PMAs, you can raise the issue to management.

### Q: Are there any costs associated with pre-submissions?

Susannah Gilbert: There are no costs associated with Q-Submissions or Pre-Submissions at this time.

### Q: Is there guidance on in-person vs. teleconference meetings?

Susannah Gilbert: That tends to be a personal decision. Some people prefer face-to-face interaction with the review team and potentially showing a demo. Others find teleconferences easier to schedule and prefer those. Everyone has their own preference.

### Q: Is Q-Sub information confidential? Is it accessible through FOIA?

Susannah Gilbert: All the information submitted is considered confidential. For questions about Freedom of Information Act accessibility, contact FDA at the email addresses provided.

### Q: Can meetings be longer than 60 minutes?

Susannah Gilbert: Typically meetings are set for one hour based on our experience that we have the most productive conversations in that amount of time. There may be exceptions depending on the situation. But typically, it is better to have a one-hour meeting so that it is more efficient, and everybody stays focused.

### Q: Do you need to complete all risk analysis activities before submitting a Pre-Sub for V&V feedback?

Susannah Gilbert: It depends on the question you're asking. The only information that needs to be submitted is relevant to the specific questions. You wouldn't have to complete all the testing or all the data collection beforehand as long as it's sufficient information for the review team to understand what you're asking and provide feedback.

### Q: What is the expected timeframe for FDA response after submission of meeting minutes?

Susannah Gilbert: Usually there's a 30-day turnaround for FDA to review meeting minutes and then respond.
