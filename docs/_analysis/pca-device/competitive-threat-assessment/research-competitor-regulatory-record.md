# Research — Competitor Regulatory & Safety Record (public sources)

> _Competitor facts researched from public sources. The GlobalLogic devices referenced by the parent analysis are demo fabrications and are NOT researched here._

## Method + limits

**Date of research:** 2026-08-05. All "as of" statements are as of that date.

**Sources queried (in priority order):**

1. **openFDA API** (`api.fda.gov`) — `device/510k`, `device/recall`, `device/enforcement` endpoints. This is the machine-readable projection of the FDA 510(k), CDRH recall, and enforcement-report databases. Used for every K-number, decision date, applicant, product code, recall number, and recall classification in this document. No K-number below was typed from memory; each was returned by an API query.
2. **fda.gov** — warning letters, recall/early-alert pages, guidance pages, the Infusion Pump Improvement Initiative page. Retrieved with `curl` (see limits).
3. **SEC EDGAR + SEC newsroom** — BD FY2025 Form 10-K (period ended 2025-09-30) read directly for consent-decree and warning-letter language; SEC enforcement press release read directly.
4. **Company press releases** (BD, Baxter, Fresenius Kabi, Eitan Medical, Moog, ICU Medical) and trade press (MedTech Dive, Fierce Biotech, Drug Delivery Business, Star Tribune) — used only where a primary source was unavailable, and graded accordingly.

**What I could not access:**

- `accessdata.fda.gov` HTML front-ends (`classification.cfm`, `pmn.cfm`, `res.cfm`) returned HTTP 404 to the fetch tool. **Workaround:** all the underlying data was obtained from the openFDA API instead, which is the same source data. No claim in this document depends on an unreachable accessdata page.
- **MAUDE adverse-event counts** were not systematically pulled. Complaint/injury/death counts quoted below come from FDA recall notices, not from an independent MAUDE query. Treat them as "as reported by the firm to FDA at the time of the recall notice."
- **SEC full-text search** was rate-limited on repeat calls; the BD 10-K and the SEC press release were successfully retrieved, but ICU Medical's 10-K was not read directly (its facts are graded accordingly).

**Two structural caveats that shape how the numbers below should be read:**

- **Recall "records" ≠ recall "events."** The FDA enforcement database emits one record per affected product/catalogue number. A single recall event routinely fans out into dozens of records. Firm-level Class I *record* counts below are therefore **not** a count of distinct recall events, and are **not** comparable across firms with different SKU granularity. They are included only as a coarse signal.
- **Firm-name searches are broader than the pump business.** A search on `recalling_firm:"Baxter Healthcare"` returns that firm's entire device portfolio, not just infusion pumps. Where a count is portfolio-wide, it is labelled as such.

## Per-competitor narrative

### BD — Alaris

BD is the incumbent in hospital PCA and it is the competitor whose regulatory position is simultaneously the strongest and the most fragile.

**The saga, dated.** The SEC order (claim 18) puts the origin in **2016**: BD knew Alaris software changes required clearance, lacked the data, and shipped anyway. By January 2019 it had catalogued **more than 25 software flaws** in its highest-harm category. It disclosed to FDA in October 2019, FDA rejected BD's proposal to keep selling, and shipping stopped (claim 19). The Consent Decree — inherited from Cardinal Health 303 in 2007 and broadened in 2009 (claim 13) — was already in force throughout. FDA's December 2021 non-compliance letter found BD's corrective actions inadequate on complaint handling, CAPA, design verification, and MDR (claim 15).

**Return to market: July 2023, not February 2024.** K211218 cleared on **2023-07-21** after a **27-month** review (claim 8). The brief's "approximately Feb 2024" is not supported by the FDA record; it most likely reflects BD's *fiscal 2024* shipping and revenue restart, which BD announced alongside the clearance (claims 9–10). A follow-on clearance, **K243855**, landed **2025-04-25** after only four months (claim 11) — the acceleration is itself informative about BD's restored standing with the review division.

**It is not over.** Three things say the file is still open:

1. BD's own FY2025 10-K says remediation will be "substantially complete over the next calendar year" (claim 12) — i.e. roughly **calendar 2026**, about seven years after the shipping halt.
2. FDA's review of BD's response to the **May 2024** Form 483 observations on the *Infusion* QMS was **still ongoing** at the FY2025 filing (claim 16), with the Consent Decree's $15k/day/$15M-per-year penalty machinery live behind it (claim 14).
3. The Class I recalls have not stopped. Since return-to-market: the connectivity-layer APR software defect (Feb 2025), pump-set performance (Jul and Sep 2025), and the dropped-module bezel hazard with two serious injuries (Oct 2025) — claims 22–24.

One frequently-mis-cited fact worth pinning: the **November 2024 warning letter is about Pyxis dispensing, not infusion** (claim 17). Attributing it to the pump business overstates BD's exposure.

**The PCA-specific product.** BD's differentiator is architectural rather than regulatory: the Alaris platform integrates the PCA module with EtCO2 and SpO2 modules, and **PCA Pause** auto-suspends the PCA infusion when a patient's capnography or oximetry crosses a hospital-set threshold (claim 26). That is a closed-loop opioid-induced-respiratory-depression safeguard on the same chassis as the pump. It is also directly exposed: the September 2023 Monoject syringe-compatibility Class I recall named the **PCA Module 8120** specifically (claim 20).

**Corporate posture.** BD spun off Biosciences & Diagnostic Solutions in February 2026 and retained infusion inside BD Connected Care (claim 27). Infusion is a keeper, not a divestiture candidate.

### ICU Medical / Smiths Medical

ICU Medical is the direct ambulatory-PCA competitor — the CADD family — and it currently carries the **worst open regulatory finding in the field**.

**What the Smiths acquisition brought.** The January 2022 deal (claim 28) transferred the CADD-Solis / CADD-Legacy ambulatory infusion franchise, plus the Medfusion syringe pumps, to ICU Medical. That is the asset that competes head-on in ambulatory PCA.

**The April 2025 warning letter.** FDA's letter CMS 702535 (claims 29–32) is unusually severe in kind, not just tone. FDA holds the **Medfusion 4000** and the **CADD Solis VIP** to be **adulterated and misbranded** — not because of a manufacturing defect, but because ICU Medical modified them in ways that could significantly affect safety or effectiveness and **never filed the 510(k)**. Three details make it worse:

- FDA states in its own voice that the CADD Solis VIP's current clearance is **K111275, dated 2013-02-01** (claim 31). The flagship ambulatory-PCA pump is running on a **13-year-old** clearance while shipping materially changed software.
- The unfiled changes were the **remedy for a Class I recall** (claim 32) — ICU pushed corrective software into the field, addressing hazards that had already caused a Class I event, without the required submission.
- FDA notes ICU Medical's **own procedure** said a 510(k) was required, and rejects the mitigation ICU attempted (a label statement that the software had not been FDA-reviewed) as "not sufficient."

**The recall record backs it up.** The December 2023 Medfusion 4000 Class I covered **50,743 units** (claim 33). Then on a single day — **2025-04-10** — three Class I recalls landed on the CADD-Solis family at once: thermal damage, false upstream occlusion alarms, and wireless-module alarms interrupting active infusions (claim 35). Two of those recalls name the **HPCA and HSPCA** configurations, i.e. the PCA variants. The wireless recall's scope was still being widened as recently as **2026-05-27**.

**Where ICU is investing.** Three LVP clearances in two years — Plum Duo (2023) and Plum Solo/Duo Precision (April 2025), claim 37 — against **zero** new PCA clearances since 2016 (claim 38). The capital is going to large-volume, not to PCA.

**On the divestiture question.** The brief's premise of an announced strategic review or sale of the Smiths Medical business is **not confirmed** (claim 40). The 2024–2025 divestiture was **IV Solutions** — a controlling 60% interest sold to Otsuka in May 2025 — which is a different business. Reporting on ICU's 2026 posture describes Smiths *integration* nearing completion, not disposal. This premise should be corrected in the parent analysis before it propagates.

### Baxter

Baxter is the **most regulatorily active filer** in the field and the least present in PCA.

Eight FRN clearances between 2022 and 2025 (claims 41–44) across two platform generations: the legacy SIGMA Spectrum / Spectrum IQ line kept current with repeated clearances, and the new **Novum IQ** platform built out syringe-first (Aug 2022), then LVP (Mar 2024), then a syringe refresh (Sep 2024). Dose IQ is the drug-library/DERS layer spanning both (claim 45). That cadence — a clearance roughly every six months — is the profile of a company that has normalized 510(k) filing as routine maintenance rather than treating it as an event.

Its safety record is not clean: the August 2023 Class I false-upstream-occlusion recall (131 complaints, 3 serious injuries, claim 46) and the March 2025 missing-motor-mounting-screw alert (claim 47). The latter is a **service-process** failure rather than a design defect, which is a different and arguably more tractable class of problem.

Baxter has effectively **exited dedicated PCA**. Its only PCA-coded clearances — Syndeo PCA (2003) and the Infusor patient-control modules (2000, 2005) — are all more than twenty years old (claim 49).

### B. Braun

B. Braun's Infusomat Space carries the **only patient death** located in this research across competitor pump recalls: the November 2023 Class I for occlusion alarms firing with no occlusion present, stopping delivery of vasopressors — 51 reports, 1 injury, 1 death (claim 51). The correction was then **expanded in August 2024** (claim 52), a nine-month lag on a Class I event with a fatality.

More striking is the clearance silence: **B. Braun has zero FRN-primary 510(k) clearances between 2020 and mid-2026** (claim 53). Its only PCA-coded clearance is Perfusor Space from 2009 (claim 50). The US clearance status of **Infusomat Space Plus** specifically could not be confirmed (claim 54), and no warning letter or consent decree was found (claim 55) — though absence in a search is weak evidence and both gaps are flagged below.

### Fresenius Kabi

Fresenius Kabi bought Ivenix in **May 2022** for $240M upfront plus milestones (claim 57), two months after clearing the Agilia VP and SP systems (claim 58). It claims Agilia Connect was the **first pump cleared following AAMI TIR101** (claim 59), which — if it holds — is a genuine regulatory-craft differentiator worth verifying independently.

Since March 2022, nothing: **no Fresenius or Ivenix FRN clearance appears in the 2020+ window after that date** (claim 60). The Ivenix LVP's own clearance predates the window and its K-number was not verified (claim 61). Fresenius has no PCA presence in the record at all.

### Eitan Medical and the ambulatory/low-cost tier

This is where the *new* activity is, and it is the tier that matters most for ambulatory PCA:

- **Eitan Medical** cleared **Avoset (K213744) on 2023-03-10** (claim 64). This corrects a common secondary-source claim that Avoset is still "under development" — it has been US-cleared for over three years. Sapphire cleared as K192860 in November 2020 under the Q Core Medical name (claim 63).
- **Moog / Zevex** cleared the CURLIN 8000 **twice in five months** — K232793 (May 2024) and K242660 (Oct 2024), claim 66 — for an all-new multi-therapy home-infusion platform (claim 67). This is the most active ambulatory-infusion clearance program in the dataset.
- **Zyno Medical** competes explicitly on **low cost** in the same ambulatory/home space (claim 68, K-number unverified), and issued a Z-800-series software recall in May 2025 (claim 69).
- Adjacent but instructive: **Deka R&D** (three Remunity clearances, 2025–2026), **Koru Medical** (four, 2021–2026), and **Iradimed** (MRI-compatible) are all filing steadily (claim 70). The new-entrant energy in infusion is going into **drug-specific, home, and niche-environment** pumps.

### The PCA segment itself

The headline number: **31 MEA-primary 510(k)s in the entire history of the code, and the most recent is from August 2017** (claims 2–3). Nine years without a single PCA-coded clearance.

That number must be read with claim 6 in hand. PCA functionality **is** being cleared — the Alaris system clearances include the PCA module and carry FRN as primary code. So the drought is not "no PCA innovation reached market"; it is that **no sponsor has filed a submission whose primary identity is a PCA pump** since 2017. Segment structure has shifted from standalone-PCA-device to PCA-as-a-module-on-a-platform (BD) or PCA-as-a-mode-on-an-ambulatory-pump (CADD, ambIT, CURLIN).

Consolidating or attracting entrants? On this evidence: **consolidating at the hospital tier, fragmenting at the ambulatory tier.** Hospital PCA has two credible players (BD Alaris PCA module; ICU Medical's LifeCare PCA, with no new clearance since 2016 and no confirmed EOL). Ambulatory PCA has more names than hospital PCA and more recent clearances — Eitan, Moog, Zyno, Summit — but all at lower price points and in home/alternate-site channels.

## Threat implications (OPINION — labelled)

Everything in this section is my analytical judgment. It has no source because it is inference about competitive posture, not a reportable fact. Grade: **OPINION** throughout.

**1. The PCA segment's regulatory bar is high and rising, and the incumbents are showing they cannot clear it consistently.** Every major competitor has taken a Class I recall on an infusion pump in the last three years, and three of the five have taken one in the last eighteen months. Two of the recurring failure modes — **false occlusion alarms** (Baxter Aug 2023, B. Braun Nov 2023, ICU Apr 2025) and **software-update-induced regressions** (Baxter Aug 2023, ICU Dec 2023) — are segment-wide, not firm-specific. Any new entrant should expect FDA to probe occlusion-detection verification and software-change control hardest, because that is where the field keeps failing.

**2. ICU Medical's warning letter is the most exploitable competitive fact in this document, and also the most dangerous precedent.** Exploitable: FDA has publicly declared the CADD Solis VIP adulterated and misbranded, and its clearance dates to 2013. That is a real opening in ambulatory PCA. Dangerous: the *mechanism* of the finding — shipping a Class I recall remedy as a software update without a 510(k) — is a trap any connected-pump manufacturer can fall into, because the pressure to remediate fast directly opposes the obligation to file first. The defensible posture is a change-control procedure that treats "does this change the risk-control measures?" as a filing trigger, and the discipline to hold the release until the submission is in. FDA's rejection of the label-disclosure workaround forecloses the obvious shortcut.

**3. BD's moat in hospital PCA is clinical architecture, not regulatory standing.** PCA Pause with integrated EtCO2/SpO2 is a hard feature to match, because it requires the monitoring modules to sit on the same platform as the pump and to be trusted enough to stop an opioid infusion. Competing on regulatory record alone will not displace it. But BD's record is genuinely encumbered — an active consent decree, an open FDA review of May-2024 Infusion QMS observations, remediation still incomplete in its seventh year, and four Class I events since returning to market. A challenger's credible pitch is **"the safety architecture, without the compliance history."**

**4. The 9-year MEA drought is an opportunity and a warning at the same time.** Opportunity: a sponsor filing a genuinely PCA-first submission enters a review queue with no recent precedent crowding it, and the "new entrant in a stagnant category" story is real. Warning: nine years of no filings under a code is also what a **dying category** looks like. The honest reading is that the *device category* is consolidating into platforms and ambulatory pumps, and a standalone-PCA product identity may be commercially disadvantaged even where it is regulatorily clean. The safer strategic frame is PCA-as-a-capability on a platform, matching how BD and ICU actually structure their filings.

**5. Cybersecurity is now the fastest-moving gate, and it moved twice in eight months.** The governing guidance was reissued in **June 2025** and again in **February 2026** (claim 75). For a connected pump, this is the requirement most likely to be stale in a submission plan written even a year ago. Any competitive-threat assessment should treat §524B conformance as a **date-stamped** obligation and re-check the governing edition at each submission gate, not once at planning time.

**6. Where the real competitive energy is: ambulatory and drug-specific, not hospital LVP.** Moog cleared CURLIN 8000 twice in five months; Deka cleared three Remunity variants in eighteen months; Koru cleared four devices in five years; Eitan has Avoset cleared and available. Meanwhile B. Braun cleared nothing in six years and Fresenius nothing since March 2022. If the threat model assumes the danger comes from the big five, it is looking at the wrong tier — the pressure on ambulatory PCA pricing and feature velocity is coming from Moog, Eitan, Zyno, and Summit.

**7. Two premises in the brief need correcting before they propagate.** The Alaris return-to-market clearance is **July 2023 (K211218)**, not February 2024 (claim 9). And there is **no confirmed ICU Medical strategic review or sale of the Smiths Medical business** — the 2024–25 divestiture was IV Solutions to Otsuka, an unrelated asset (claim 40). Both errors would, if carried forward, materially mis-time and mis-read the competitive window.
